Math · MA.6.AR.3.2 I Do

Possession 1: Pricing the Booster Table (Unit Rate)

The Booster Club is selling team T-shirts at tonight's game: 25 shirts for $375. To post a single price tag, they need the unit rate — the price for just one shirt.

Divide the total by the number of items: $375 ÷ 25 = $15 per shirt. That's the unit rate — one quantity for one unit of the other.

Unit rates also let you compare two different deals fairly. The concession stand sells popcorn two ways: Vendor A: 8 bags for $24, Vendor B: 10 bags for $25.

Vendor A: $24 ÷ 8 = $3.00 per bag. Vendor B: $25 ÷ 10 = $2.50 per bag. Even though Vendor B's total is higher, its unit rate is lower — the better deal per bag.

Test Tactic

"Which is the better deal" questions are unit-rate questions in disguise. Find the price per single unit for each option before comparing — never compare totals directly.

Possession 2: Reading the Free-Throw Line (Percentages)

Jasmine has attempted 20 free throws this season and made 15. Her free-throw percentage tells you how often she scores from the line.

Divide makes by attempts, then multiply by 100: 15 ÷ 20 = 0.75, and 0.75 × 100 = 75%.

Test Tactic

"Made X of Y attempts" always means divide the smaller number (makes) by the larger number (attempts), then convert to a percent. Attempts always go on the bottom.

Possession 3: Points Per Game (PPG)

Over the last four games, the Comets scored 84, 92, 78, and 86 points. Sportscasters call the average score per game PPG — points per game.

Add every game's total, divide by the number of games: (84+92+78+86) ÷ 4 = 340 ÷ 4 = 85 PPG.

Test Tactic

PPG is the same operation as finding a mean — just applied to game scores instead of a generic data set. If you can find a mean, you can find a PPG.

Possession 4: Reading the Whole Stat Line

Real box scores mix all three ideas together: unit rates, percentages, and averages, side by side. The skill FAST actually tests is knowing which one a question wants before you calculate anything.

"Price per..." or "which is the better rate" → unit rate.

"Made X of Y" or "what fraction became a percent" → percentage.

"Average," "typical," or "PPG" → mean.

Test Tactic

Underline the exact phrase that signals which operation a word problem wants before you touch a single number. Guessing the operation first is where most points get lost on mixed-review items.

ELA · ELA.6.R.1.2 I Do

What a Theme Actually Is

A theme is the lesson or bigger idea a story teaches through what happens to its characters — not just what the story is about, but what it means.

"Maria spent all afternoon trying to fix the bike alone, getting more frustrated with every failed attempt. When she finally asked her neighbor for help, they had it running in ten minutes."

This story is about a broken bike. Its theme is bigger: asking for help isn't a failure — it's often the faster, smarter choice.

As you read "Full-Court Press," watch for what the main character learns by the end — not just what happens, but what it teaches her.

Similes vs. Metaphors

Writers use figurative language to make a moment feel more vivid than a plain description would.

A simile compares two things using "like" or "as": "the crowd roared like thunder."

A metaphor compares two things directly, without "like" or "as": "the crowd was thunder." Same comparison, no signal word.

ELA · ELA.6.C.3.1 I Do

Joining Sentences With a Semicolon

Two closely related complete sentences can also be joined with a semicolon instead of a comma-plus-conjunction — no connecting word needed, just the semicolon between two complete ideas.

Separate: "Jasmine had the ball. The defenders closed in fast."

Combined: "Jasmine had the ball; the defenders closed in fast."

A semicolon can only join two sentences that could each stand alone — if either half isn't a complete sentence by itself, a semicolon is the wrong tool.
