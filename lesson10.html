Math · MA.6.GR.2.1 I Do

Plot 1: A Triangular Garden Bed

One of the garden beds is shaped like a triangle: base 8 feet, height 6 feet.

Area of a triangle = (base × height) ÷ 2 = (8 × 6) ÷ 2 = 48 ÷ 2 = 24 square feet.

Test Tactic

A triangle is always exactly HALF of the rectangle that would surround it — multiply base times height like a rectangle, then divide by 2. Forgetting that last step is the single most common triangle-area mistake.

Plot 2: A Composite Garden Shape

One plot combines a rectangle (6 ft by 8 ft) with a triangular extension (base 6 ft, height 4 ft) attached to one side.

Rectangle area: 6 × 8 = 48 sq ft. Triangle area: (6 × 4) ÷ 2 = 12 sq ft. Total area = 48 + 12 = 60 square feet.

Test Tactic

Break any composite figure into shapes you already know how to measure, find each piece's area separately, then add every piece together — never try to measure the whole irregular shape at once.

Plot 3: One More Triangle

A workshop's roof cross-section is triangular: base 10 feet, height 7 feet. Area = (10 × 7) ÷ 2 = 70 ÷ 2 = 35 square feet.

Test Tactic

The formula never changes, no matter what the triangle is measuring — garden bed, roof, or anything else. Base times height, divided by 2, every time.

Plot 4: Reading the Whole Site Plan

A real site plan mixes plain triangles and composite shapes together. The skill FAST tests is knowing when a shape needs ONE formula versus needing to be broken into pieces first.

A single triangle → (base × height) ÷ 2, one calculation.

A composite shape → split into a rectangle and a triangle (or more), find each area separately, then add them together.

Test Tactic

Forgetting to divide a triangle's base-times-height by 2 — or forgetting to ADD the triangle piece onto a composite figure's rectangle piece — are the two most common errors on this benchmark.

ELA · ELA.6.R.3.3 I Do

Comparing the Same Place, Two Different Times

When a passage compares something across time, look for what stayed the same and what actually changed — the comparison usually reveals the passage's real point.

"Twenty years ago, the corner lot was an empty gravel patch. Today it's a playground with a mural, a bench, and a little free library. The lot never got bigger — what neighbors decided to do with it did."

What stayed the same: the size of the lot. What changed: everything about how it's used.
