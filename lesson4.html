Math · MA.6.NSO.2.1 I Do

Budget Item 1: Pricing the Tablecloths

The committee needs 45 tablecloths for the vendor tables, and each one costs $3.25. To find the total cost, multiply the quantity by the price.

45 × 3.25 — multiply as if there were no decimal point first: 45 × 325 = 14,625. Then count the decimal places in the original numbers (two, from 3.25) and place the decimal point two spots from the right: $146.25.

Test Tactic

"Total cost for [quantity] at [price] each" is always multiplication. Multiply first without worrying about the decimal, then place the decimal point last by counting total decimal digits in the original problem.

Budget Item 2: Splitting the Banner Cost

The committee has $180 to spend on 8 identical banner signs. To find the cost of just one, divide the total by the quantity.

$180 ÷ 8 — divide as usual: 180 ÷ 8 = 22.5. That's $22.50 per banner.

Test Tactic

"Cost per item," "split evenly," or "each" all signal division — the total always goes on top, the number of items on the bottom.

Budget Item 3: Mapping the Booths

Diego draws the festival layout on a grid so every vendor knows exactly where to set up. The origin (0,0) sits at the corner of Main Street: x is distance down the block, y is distance across toward the park.

Booth A sits at (3, 4), Booth B at (3, 9) — same x-coordinate, different y. Since the x-values match, the distance between them is just the difference in the y-values: 9 − 4 = 5 feet.

Diego also marks off a rectangular info-tent zone from x=0 to x=6 and y=0 to y=8 — a rectangle 6 feet by 8 feet. Area = 6 × 8 = 48 square feet. Perimeter = 2(6+8) = 28 feet of rope to mark the boundary.

Test Tactic

When two points share the same x-coordinate, subtract the y-values for the distance between them. When they share the same y-coordinate, subtract the x-values instead.

Budget Item 4: Reading the Whole Ledger

A real budget spreadsheet mixes multiplication, division, and mapping side by side. The skill FAST tests is spotting which operation a line item wants before touching any numbers.

"Total cost for [quantity] at [price] each" → multiply.

"Cost per item" or "split evenly" → divide.

Same x-coordinate → subtract y-values. Same y-coordinate → subtract x-values.

Test Tactic

Underline the exact phrase that signals the operation before calculating anything — guessing the operation first is where most points get lost on mixed-review items.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.1 I Do

What Text Features Actually Do

Text features — headers, boxed stats, bullet lists, subheadings — aren't decoration. Each one exists to help a reader find or absorb specific information faster than plain paragraph text would.

A recipe blog could bury the ingredient list inside a paragraph. Instead, it's almost always a separate bulleted list — because a cook needs to scan it quickly while cooking, not read it like a story.

As you read Diego's column, notice the boxed stat section. Ask yourself: what job is that box doing that a paragraph couldn't do as well?

Arcadia Community Bulletin · Guest Column

Why Hometown Ties Matters

By Diego Reyes, Festival Planning Committee

Every fall, Hometown Ties turns three blocks of Main Street into Arcadia's biggest gathering of the year — and after two years helping plan it, I've seen exactly why the festival deserves the city's continued support.

By the Numbers
Vendors hosted: 34 · Funds raised for school programs: $8,200 · Estimated attendance: 1,600

The festival is one of the only times all year that neighbors from every part of town end up standing in the same line for kettle corn, comparing notes on gardens, and actually meeting the people who live three streets over.

Last year's festival raised over eight thousand dollars for after-school programs at Arcadia Elementary — money the school district's regular budget doesn't cover, and money that pays for real supplies kids use every week.

For small vendors who can't afford a storefront rent, a single festival weekend can bring in more new customers than an entire month of regular business — exposure they'd have no other way to reach.

None of that happens by accident. It happens because the city keeps funding it, block captains keep organizing it, and residents keep showing up — which is exactly why Hometown Ties is worth protecting, not cutting.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.4 I Do

Matching Evidence to the Right Reason

A strong argument backs each reason with its own specific evidence — not one piece of evidence stretched to cover everything. Part of reading an argument well is matching each detail to the exact reason it supports.

Diego's column has three separate reasons. The "eight thousand dollars for after-school programs" detail supports the economic reason specifically — it doesn't say anything about neighbors meeting each other or vendors getting exposure. Matching evidence to the right reason, not just any reason, is what FAST evidence-based items actually test.

FAST-Format Item You Do — Scored

Prove the Reasons

Evidence-Based Response, Part 2: Which detail from the passage best supports that first reason?

Test Tactic

Evidence-based items are graded as a pair — Part 2 only counts if Part 1 is right too. Pin down which reason before you go hunting for its specific proof.

ELA · ELA.6.C.3.1 I Do

Fixing a Fragment

A sentence fragment is missing a subject, a verb, or is a dependent clause left standing alone, disconnected from a complete sentence. A dependent clause starting with a word like because, although, since, or even though can't stand by itself — it has to attach to an independent clause.

Fragment: "Because the festival raised so much money for schools." (This starts an idea but never finishes it.)

Fixed: "Because the festival raised so much money for schools, the district bought new classroom supplies." (Now it's attached to a complete idea.)
