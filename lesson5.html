Record 1: Reading the Growth Numbers (Exponents)

The Bright Water Nation's cultural center is expanding its craft workshop room — right now it's a square, 4 feet by 4 feet. Multiplying a number by itself, 4 × 4, can be written as 4² (four squared, or four to the second power). 4² = 16 square feet.

The exponent tells you how many times to multiply the base by itself. A storage crate shaped like a cube, 3 feet on every side, has a volume of 3 × 3 × 3 = 3³ (three cubed) = 27 cubic feet.

Test Tactic

The small raised number is the exponent — it tells you how many copies of the base to multiply, not what to multiply the base BY once. 4² is 4×4, never 4×2.

Record 2: Sorting the Displays (Prime Factorization)

The center has 36 handwoven baskets ready for display. To find every possible way to arrange them in equal rows, break 36 down into its prime factors — prime numbers that multiply together to make the original number.

36 = 6 × 6 = (2 × 3) × (2 × 3) = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3. Written with exponents: 2² × 3². Every factor in that list is prime — it can't be broken down any further.

Test Tactic

Keep breaking a number into two factors until every factor left is prime — a factor tree is the easiest way to track it. If a branch ends on a number that's still even, still divisible by 3, 5, or another prime, it isn't finished yet.

Record 3: Telling a Statistical Question From a Fact

A statistical question is one where you'd expect the answer to vary — because it's really asking about many different people, events, or times, not one fixed thing.

"How old is the center's oldest building?" has exactly one correct answer — not statistical.

"How many people attend the center's craft workshops each month?" will come out different every time you check — different months bring different crowds. That variability is what makes it statistical.

Test Tactic

Ask yourself: if I asked this question about many different people or checked it on many different occasions, would I expect different answers? Yes — statistical. No, there's only one true answer — not statistical.

Record 4: Reading the Whole Ledger

Today's three ideas — exponents, prime factorization, and statistical questions — show up together in real record-keeping. The skill FAST actually tests is recognizing which one a question is really asking about.

A small raised number next to a base → exponent, meaning repeated multiplication.

"Break down into primes" or "arrange in equal groups" → prime factorization.

"Would the answer vary if I asked again?" → statistical question.

Test Tactic

Not every prime factor of a number is itself odd or unfamiliar — 2 is prime too, and it's usually the first factor to pull out of an even number.

ELA · ELA.6.R.1.3 I Do

When a Story Has More Than One Narrator

Oral storytelling — stories passed down by being told and retold rather than written — has been a way many families and communities, including many Indigenous nations, have carried history and memory across generations. One thing that happens naturally when a story is retold: different tellers bring their own point of view.

When a story has multiple narrators, each one tells events through their own memory, their own perspective, their own point of view. That doesn't make one version "the fake one" — it just means you're reading two honest accounts shaped by two different people.

As you read "The Story of the Blue Door," pay attention to who is narrating each part, and how each narrator's version differs.

Read · Literary Text

"The Story of the Blue Door"

Grandmother's Version

My grandmother tells it like this: "When I was your age, our family moved into a house with a bright blue front door. The door was so unusual for our street that neighbors started calling our house 'the blue door house' before they even learned our name. I remember standing on the porch that first night, counting the blue door houses on our street — there was exactly one. Ours."

Rosa's Version

When I tell the story now, it comes out a little different: my grandmother's family moved into a house with a blue door, and the whole neighborhood knew the house because of it. I always pictured the door as bright turquoise, though Grandma says it was more of a deep navy blue. And in my version, there were always three blue doors on the street — Grandma's, and two others down the block, which made me wonder as a kid if blue doors were just a thing everyone did that year.

Rosa's Reflection

That's the strange thing about a story passed down: it doesn't stay exactly the same. Grandma remembers one blue door on the whole street. I remember three. Neither of us is really wrong — we're just telling the same story from two different points in time, shaped by two different memories.

ELA · ELA.6.R.3.3 I Do

Comparing a Story Across Two Tellings

When the same story gets told at two different points in time, comparing them isn't about finding "the wrong version." It's about noticing exactly which details shifted — and thinking about why memory might work that way.

Grandma's telling happened close to the actual event — one detailed, specific memory: one blue door. Rosa's telling happened years later, filtered through childhood memory of hearing the story: three blue doors, a different shade of blue. Comparing them side by side is exactly the skill FAST's compare/contrast items test.

Four Words From the Story

Strategy — Context clues.

"The door was so unusual for our street that neighbors started calling our house 'the blue door house'" — the surrounding sentence describes something standing out from everything around it, pointing to what "unusual" means.

"I always pictured the door as bright turquoise" — "pictured" here means imagined or formed a mental image, since Rosa never actually saw the original door herself.

ELA · ELA.6.C.3.1 I Do

Splitting a Run-On Into Two Sentences

Not every run-on needs to be joined — sometimes the cleanest fix is splitting two complete ideas into two separate sentences with a period and a capital letter.

Run-on: "Rosa remembers three doors her grandmother remembers only one."

Fixed: "Rosa remembers three doors. Her grandmother remembers only one." Two separate, complete ideas — no comma, no conjunction needed, just a clean break.
