Data 1: Reading a Box Plot

A box plot shows the ages of visitors at the music room's open house.

Range = Max − Min = 45 − 8 = 37 (the full spread of ages).

IQR (interquartile range) = Q3 − Q1 = 30 − 15 = 15 (the spread of the middle 50% of visitors).

Test Tactic

Range always uses the two OUTER values (min and max). IQR always uses the two MIDDLE box edges (Q1 and Q3). Mixing these up is the most common box plot mistake.

Data 2: Reading a Histogram

A histogram shows how many open-house visitors fell into each age group.

The tallest bar is 20-29, with 18 visitors — that's the most common age group. Adding buckets together works too: visitors aged 30 or older = 9 + 3 = 12.

Test Tactic

The height (or the printed number) of each bar is its frequency — how many data points fall in that bucket. Read the label carefully before answering "how many."

Data 3: A Fundraiser Box Plot

A box plot of donation amounts (in dollars) at the fundraiser: Min=10, Q1=25, Median=40, Q3=60, Max=100.

The median donation was $40 — the middle value once every donation is lined up in order. Half of all donations were above $40, and half were below it.

Test Tactic

The median is always the line INSIDE the box, never one of the outer whisker ends. Don't confuse it with the min or max.

Data 4: Reading the Full Report

A real program report mixes box plots and histograms together. FAST tests whether you can pull the right specific value from each display, not just recognize what type of chart it is.

Box plot → Range = Max − Min. IQR = Q3 − Q1. Median = the line inside the box.

Histogram → each bar's height (or label) is a frequency — a count, not a percent.

ELA · ELA.6.R.3.4 I Do

Three Ways a Speaker Tries to Convince You

A persuasive speech makes a claim, then tries to convince the audience using different kinds of appeals:

Logic: facts, numbers, and reasoning. "The program has run for ten years and never missed a season."

Emotion: feelings, personal stories, vivid moments. "I remember being scared on my first day, and how much that fear mattered to someone."

Credibility: the speaker's own experience or trustworthiness. "I've volunteered here myself for six years, so I've seen this firsthand."

As you read the speech below, watch for all three.

Read · Persuasive Speech

Why the Music Room Should Bear Her Name

Community Council Meeting · Public Comment

My name is Malik Turner, and for three years I sat in Ms. Marsh's after-school music program every single Tuesday. I'm not here as an expert. I'm here as one of dozens of kids this program changed, and I'm asking this council to name the room after the person who built it.

The numbers alone make the case: over fifteen years, the after-school program she started has served more than three hundred students, and every single year it ran entirely on volunteer hours, not a paid staff position. That's not luck. That's one person's sustained commitment.

I still remember being nine years old, too nervous to play in front of anyone, and her telling me it was fine to mess up in her room — that messing up was actually how you got better. I don't think I'd still be playing an instrument if she hadn't said that.

Naming a room doesn't cost the district anything. What it does is tell the next nine-year-old walking in nervous that someone like them built this place, and someone like them can build the next one.

ELA · ELA.6.V.1.1 / 1.2 / 1.3 I Do

Four Words From the Speech

Strategy — Context clues.

"one person's sustained commitment" — paired with "over fifteen years," "sustained" points to something that continued steadily over a long time.

"it ran entirely on volunteer hours, not a paid staff position" — the contrast with "paid" points to what "volunteer" means: unpaid, given freely.
