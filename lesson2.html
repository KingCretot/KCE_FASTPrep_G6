Math · MA.6.NSO.3.1 I Do

Problem 1: Sorting the Parts Bin (GCF)

Ada's workshop has two bins of leftover gears from finished jobs: Bin A has 18 gears, Bin B has 24 gears. She wants to sort every gear into identical small kits — same number of gears from each bin in every kit — with none left over, using the largest kit size possible.

That largest possible group size is the Greatest Common Factor (GCF): the biggest number that divides evenly into both totals.

Factors of 18: 1, 2, 3, 6, 9, 18

Factors of 24: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 12, 24

The factors both lists share: 1, 2, 3, 6. The largest shared factor is 6 — so GCF(18, 24) = 6. Ada can make kits of 6 gears each: three kits from Bin A, four kits from Bin B, nothing left over.

Test Tactic

"Largest group with none left over" is the GCF signal every time. List factors of both numbers, circle what they share, take the biggest shared one.

Problem 2: Scheduling the Machines (LCM)

Two of Ada's machines need routine oiling on different schedules: the big loom every 4 days, the small loom every 6 days. She oils both today. When is the next day she'll oil both on the same day again?

That next shared day is the Least Common Multiple (LCM): the smallest number that both schedules land on.

Multiples of 4: 4, 8, 12, 16, 20, 24…

Multiples of 6: 6, 12, 18, 24, 30…

The first number both lists share is 12 — so LCM(4, 6) = 12. Twelve days from today, both machines line up again.

Test Tactic

"When will two repeating things line up again" is the LCM signal — the opposite direction from GCF. GCF breaks a big number down into shared pieces; LCM builds up to a shared multiple. Mixing the two up is the single most common mistake on this benchmark.

Math · MA.6.DP.1.2 I Do

Problem 3: Reading Ada's Own Numbers (Mean & Median)

Devon found Ada's old logbook, tracking hours worked per week one month: 6, 8, 4, 8, 4. That's a real data set — the same kind FAST asks you to analyze.

Mean (the average): add every value, divide by how many there are. (6+8+4+8+4) ÷ 5 = 30 ÷ 5 = 6 hours per week.

Median (the middle value): put the data in order first — 4, 4, 6, 8, 8 — then take the middle number. With 5 values, that's the 3rd one: 6. Order matters here; skip that step and you'll grab the wrong number.

Test Tactic

Mean and median can land on the same number, like they did here — that's not a mistake, it just means this data set happens to be balanced. Never skip finding both just because one already matches your guess.

Math · MA.6.DP.1.2 I Do

Problem 4: The Patent Years (Mode & Range)

One more entry from the logbook: patents Ada filed each year over five years — 3, 5, 3, 6, 3.

Mode (the value that shows up most): 3 appears three times — more than any other value. Mode = 3.

Range (the spread): highest minus lowest. 6 − 3 = 3, a small, steady spread — Ada's output didn't swing wildly year to year.

Test Tactic

Mean, median, mode, and range each answer a different question. "What's typical?" → mean or median. "What repeats most?" → mode. "How spread out?" → range. Match the question's wording to the right measure before you calculate anything.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.2 I Do

What a Central Idea Actually Is

A central idea is the one big point an informational passage is built to prove. Every other sentence in the passage exists to support that one point — it's the claim, not just a topic.

"The Lincoln Middle School garden club built three raised beds this spring. They also started a compost bin, invited a local farmer to speak, and won a small grant for new tools. Through steady effort, the club turned an empty lot into a thriving space for the whole school to use."

The raised beds, the compost bin, the farmer visit, the grant — each of those is a supporting detail. The last sentence is the central idea: it's the one claim all those details are proving.

As you read Devon's profile of Ada Freeman, look for the sentence that every other sentence is working to support. That's almost always the central idea FAST is asking about.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.3 I Do

Why a Writer Chooses What to Include

Writers don't include every fact they find — they choose the details that do a specific job. Ask: what does this detail make me understand or feel that the passage wouldn't work without?

A profile could just list dates and patent numbers. Instead, Devon's profile mentions that Ada started as a fourteen-year-old apprentice before describing her patents. Why include the apprentice detail at all?

Because it shows the achievement wasn't instant — it makes the later success feel earned, built on years of learning rather than a lucky idea. That's the author's purpose behind including it.

ELA · ELA.6.C.3.1 I Do

Joining Two Complete Ideas

When two complete sentences are closely related, you can join them into one compound sentence using a comma plus a coordinating conjunction: for, and, nor, but, or, yet, so.

Separate: "Ada solved the gear problem. She trained a dozen apprentices."

Combined: "Ada solved the gear problem, and she trained a dozen apprentices."

Notice the comma comes before the conjunction, not after. Leave the comma out and you get a run-on; put the comma in the wrong spot and it still reads as an error. This exact fix is what FAST's Editing Task Choice items test.
