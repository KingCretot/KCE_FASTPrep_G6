Station 1: Mapping the Drill Stations

Coach maps out shooting-drill stations on a coordinate grid overlaid on the practice court. The origin (0,0) sits at one corner of the baseline: x is feet along the baseline, y is feet toward half-court.

Station A sits at (2, 3), Station B at (2, 9) — same x-coordinate. The distance between them is the difference in the y-values: 9 − 3 = 6 feet.

Test Tactic

To plot a point, count right for the x-value first, then up for the y-value. Order matters — (2, 9) and (9, 2) are two completely different locations.

Station 2: Sizing the Drill Zone

Coach marks off a rectangular passing-drill zone from x=0 to x=7 and y=0 to y=5 — a rectangle 7 feet by 5 feet.

Area = length × width = 7 × 5 = 35 square feet. Perimeter = 2(length + width) = 2(7+5) = 24 feet of tape to mark the boundary.

Test Tactic

Coordinate corners give you length and width by subtracting x-values for one side and y-values for the other — from there it's the same area and perimeter formulas every time.

Station 3: One More Distance Check

Two more stations, same idea. Station H sits at (6, 3), Station I at (6, 11) — same x-coordinate, so subtract the y-values: 11 − 3 = 8 feet.

Test Tactic

Same x-coordinate — subtract the y-values. Same y-coordinate — subtract the x-values. Whichever coordinate matches tells you which pair of numbers to leave alone.

Station 4: Reading the Whole Court Map

A real court diagram mixes plotted points, distances, and rectangular zones together. The skill FAST tests is recognizing which formula a question actually wants.

Same coordinate shared → subtract the OTHER pair of values for distance.

"Area" → length × width. "Perimeter" → 2(length + width) — add the two sides first, then double it.

Test Tactic

Perimeter is one of the few formulas where the ORDER matters visually — add length and width first, inside the parentheses, then multiply by 2 last.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.2 I Do

Finding the Claim Under the Details

A central idea is the one big point an informational passage is built to prove — not just its topic, but its actual claim.

"The town library added weekend hours, a homework help desk, and a used-book donation bin this year. Visits are up thirty percent. Small, practical changes can make a public space more useful without needing a bigger budget."

The weekend hours, homework desk, and donation bin are supporting details. The last sentence is the central idea — the claim all those details are proving.

As you read about the Comets' turnaround, look for the sentence every other detail is working to support.

One More Read for Precision

Not every true sentence in an informational passage is equally important to its central idea. Some details support the claim directly — others just add color or describe a single moment along the way.

The free-throw percentage jump happened within ONE season, per the passage. The full turnaround took THREE seasons. Mixing those two timeframes up is a common comprehension slip — FAST rewards catching the difference.
