Math · MA.6.NSO.1.1 / 1.2 / 1.3 I Do

Drive 1: Reading the Field Like a Number Line

Picture the line of scrimmage as zero. Every play either moves the ball toward the Eagles' end zone (positive) or gets pushed backward (negative). A number line is just a football field with a ruler drawn on it.

Watch four real plays from the Eagles' opening drive get plotted, one at a time:

Play 1 — Run up the middle: gain of 5 yards. +5: five ticks right of zero.

Play 2 — Quarterback sacked: loss of 7 yards. −7: seven ticks left of zero.

Play 3 — Deep pass completion: gain of 12 yards. +12, the biggest move yet.

Play 4 — Holding penalty: loss of 10 yards. −10.

Absolute value is the distance from zero, ignoring direction. |−10| = 10, and |+10| would also = 10 — a 10-yard penalty and a 10-yard gain cover the same distance, even though one helps the Eagles and one hurts them.

Test Tactic

When FAST asks for "distance" or "absolute value," direction never matters. When it asks what actually happened, direction is the whole answer. Read the question twice.

Drive 2: Grading the Passing Game

Quarterback Jalen Torres has thrown 20 passes tonight and completed 15. A ratio compares those numbers exactly as they happened: 15 completions to 20 attempts, written 15:20.

15 and 20 share a factor of 5, so 15:20 reduces to 3:4. As a percent, that's 75%. Three ways to say the same thing: 15:20 = 3:4 = 75%.

Marcus, the receiver, has been targeted 10 times and caught 8.

8:10 simplifies to 4:5, or 80%. Even though Jalen has more total completions, Marcus is catching a higher percentage of what's thrown his way.

Test Tactic

Ratio questions often list numbers backward from how you'd naturally think about them. Whatever order the question states first, that's the order your ratio goes in.

Drive 3: Mapping the Field

Broadcast cameras track the field on a grid, same as a coordinate plane. We'll set the origin (0,0) at midfield on the near sideline: x is yards toward the Eagles' end zone, y is yards across the field from that sideline.

Marcus lines up at (3, 4) — 3 yards downfield, 4 yards from the sideline. A defender lines up at (3, 11) — same x-coordinate, different y. Since the x-values match, the separation between them is just the difference in the y-values: 11 − 4 = 7 yards.

Coordinates also give you shapes. The Eagles mark off a passing drill zone from x=0 to x=8 and y=0 to y=10 — a rectangle 8 yards long by 10 yards wide. Area = 8 × 10 = 80 square yards. Perimeter = 2(8+10) = 36 yards of chalk.

Test Tactic

FAST coordinate items often give you a shape by its corner points instead of stating length and width directly. Subtract the x-values for length, subtract the y-values for width, then multiply.

Drive 4: By the Numbers — the Whole Game

The Eagles' offense gained a different number of yards each quarter tonight. That's a real data set — the same kind FAST asks you to analyze.

Mean (the average): add all four values, divide by 4. (45+38+22+55) ÷ 4 = 160 ÷ 4 = 40 yards per quarter.

Median (the middle value): put the data in order — 22, 38, 45, 55 — then average the two middle numbers: (38+45)÷2 = 41.5. Notice it's close to the mean here, but not identical — they answer slightly different questions.

Range (the spread): highest minus lowest. 55 − 22 = 33 yards, a big swing between the Eagles' best and worst quarter.

Look at that Q3 bar — the shortest one. That's the same quarter Marcus dropped the pass. The numbers and the story match up.

Test Tactic

Mean, median, and range measure different things. If a question asks what a "typical" value looks like, mean or median both work. If it asks how spread out the data is, that's range — don't reach for mean out of habit.

ELA · ELA.6.R.1.1 I Do

3rd Quarter: What Actually Moves a Story Forward

A story moves forward because of how characters react to each other — that reaction changes what happens next.

"Sarah slammed the door, furious her brother had broken her trophy. But when he showed up an hour later with the pieces glued back together and offered to help her train for regionals instead, her anger turned into something closer to partnership."

The brother's action directly changed Sarah's feelings and what she does next. That shift is the plot moving. If he'd just apologized and left, the story stays stuck.

As you read "The Last Rep," watch for the exact moment one character's words change the direction of the scene. That moment is almost always the answer to a plot question on FAST.

Read · Literary Text

"The Last Rep"

Marcus sat alone on the bench, cleats still laced, staring at the scoreboard: Panthers 13, Eagles 10. Third quarter, and he'd dropped the pass that would have kept the drive alive — then got dragged four yards backward trying to make up for it on the very next play, like the field itself was punishing him twice for one mistake.

Coach Whitfield sat down beside him without a word at first. He just watched the empty part of the field for a moment before speaking.

"You know what I remember about that play?" Coach asked. "Not the drop. I remember you sprinting back downfield afterward to make the tackle on the return. Most guys sulk. You reacted instantly."

Marcus looked up, surprised. "I thought you'd be mad."

"I'm not thrilled," Coach admitted, "but a dropped ball doesn't tell me who you are. What you did the next three seconds does." He stood, offering a hand to pull Marcus up. "Next practice, same route, same ball. We run it until it's automatic. That's the only remedy for a drop — more reps, not more worrying."

Marcus took the hand, and for the first time since the fumble, the tightness in his chest eased. It wasn't the forgiveness he expected. It was something better — a plan. He glanced at the scoreboard one more time. Three points. Fifteen minutes left. Jalen had one more drive in him, and Marcus intended to be exactly where the ball needed him to be.

He jogged back onto the field already running the route in his head — not the drop, not the tackle. Just the next rep.

ELA · ELA.6.V.1.1 / 1.2 / 1.3 I Do

Two Ways to Crack a Word You Don't Know

Strategy 1 — Definition clues.

"That's the only remedy for a drop — more reps, not more worrying." Coach describes the fix right next to the word. Remedy = a fix.

Strategy 2 — Contrast clues.

"Most guys sulk. You reacted instantly." "Sulk" is contrasted with "reacted instantly" — meaning something like the opposite: staying upset and withdrawn instead of responding right away.

Word parts count too. "Automatic" breaks down into the root auto-, meaning "self." Something automatic happens by itself, without conscious thought.

ELA · ELA.6.C.3.1 / C.1.2 I Do

Combining Choppy Sentences

Two short, separate sentences can sound weak next to each other. A subordinating conjunction — although, because, when, if, since, while — joins them into one stronger sentence.

Choppy: "Marcus was nervous. He stepped onto the field."

Combined: "Although Marcus was nervous, he stepped onto the field."

This is exactly the kind of fix FAST's Editing Task Choice items test.
