Order 1: Restocking the Table

Nadia buys 3 bags of beads at $4.50 each, plus a $2.00 clasp kit. Multi-step problems need every operation the words describe, done in order.

(3 × 4.50) + 2.00 = 13.50 + 2.00 = $15.50 total. Multiply first (the repeated cost), then add the one-time extra.

Test Tactic

Read a multi-step problem twice before calculating anything. Underline each operation the words describe — "each" or "per" means multiply, "plus" or "then" means the next step comes after.

Order 2: Keeping the Bead Pattern Consistent

Nadia's necklaces use a ratio of 5 blue beads to 3 white beads. If she uses 15 blue beads, how many white beads does she need to keep the exact same pattern?

15 ÷ 5 = 3, so the pattern scaled up by a factor of 3. White beads: 3 × 3 = 9 white beads. Whatever you multiply one side by, multiply the other side by the same amount.

Test Tactic

Find the scale factor first (how many times bigger the known side got), then apply that SAME factor to the unknown side.

Order 3: Counting the Day's Profit

Nadia sells 5 bracelets at $9 each, and pays a $15 table fee for the day. Her net profit needs two steps: total sales, then subtract the fee.

(5 × 9) − 15 = 45 − 15 = $30 profit. Multiply the repeated sale first, subtract the one-time fee last.

Test Tactic

"Profit" always means total money in, minus total money spent — find both totals separately before subtracting.

Order 4: Reading the Whole Cash Box

A real market day mixes multi-step costs and ratio scaling together. FAST tests whether you can build the RIGHT sequence of operations from the words before calculating.

"[Number] at [price] each, plus [extra]" → multiply then add.

"Started with, spent, spent" → subtract each amount in order.

"If [known amount], how many [unknown]" at a fixed ratio → find the scale factor, then apply it.

Test Tactic

Every multi-step word problem has a natural order the operations happen in — follow the sentence structure, not a guess.

Reading a Poem's Structure

Poems are often built in stanzas — groups of lines, like paragraphs — and many use a rhyme scheme, a pattern of which lines rhyme with each other.

"The kettle sings, the morning's cold, / A story waits to be retold. / Steam rises up in lazy curls, / Around the kitchen light unfurls."

Line 1 rhymes with line 2 ("cold"/"retold"), and line 3 rhymes with line 4 ("curls"/"unfurls"). That pattern — two rhyming pairs in a row — is called AABB, or rhyming couplets.

As you read "Saturday on Main Street," count the stanzas and listen for the rhyme pattern.

"Saturday on Main Street"

The market wakes before the sun,
Tables rise, the day's begun.
Nadia sets her beads in rows,
Waiting for the crowd that grows.
A dollar here, a story told,
Nothing flashy, nothing bold.
Just neighbors trading what they made,
A simple kind of local trade.
By noon the square is full of sound,
Laughter, haggling, all around.
And Nadia counts her earnings twice,
Proud of every honest price.

ELA · ELA.6.R.3.2 I Do

Saying It in Your Own Words

A good paraphrase restates the same meaning in different words — nothing added that wasn't there, nothing changed that WAS there.

Original: "The kettle sings, the morning's cold." Good paraphrase: "The kettle whistles on a chilly morning." Bad paraphrase: "Someone is making tea for the whole neighborhood" — that adds a detail the line never states.

Four Words From the Poem

Strategy — Context clues.

"Laughter, haggling, all around" — paired with the busy market sounds, "haggling" describes the friendly back-and-forth bargaining over a price.

"Nothing flashy, nothing bold" — both words describe the opposite of plain, pointing to what "flashy" means: showy, meant to attract attention.
