Math · MA.6.AR.1.1 I Do

Pattern 1: Writing the Beat Count

One of Odessa's rhythm exercises has a conga drum play 3 beats every measure. If a song has m measures, the total number of beats is an algebraic expression: 3m.

The variable m stands in for a measure count we don't know yet. "3 beats per measure" becomes multiplication — the rate (3) times the variable (m).

Test Tactic

"Per," "each," or "every" paired with a rate almost always means multiply that rate by the variable. Watch for a second detail added on top — that usually means add or subtract a fixed number after the multiplication.

Math · MA.6.AR.1.3 I Do

Pattern 2: Plugging in the Measure Count

Once you know how many measures a song actually has, you can find the exact total. If the conga pattern (3m) plays across a song with 8 measures, substitute m = 8: 3(8) = 24 beats total.

Substitution just means replacing the variable with the actual number, then doing the arithmetic exactly as written.

Test Tactic

Replace the variable with its value first, then follow order of operations exactly — parentheses and multiplication before any addition or subtraction outside them.

Math · MA.6.AR.1.3 I Do

Pattern 3: Expressions With Grouping

Some patterns need parentheses. Evaluate 3(m + 2) when m = 5: follow order of operations — add inside the parentheses first, 5 + 2 = 7, then multiply: 3 × 7 = 21.

Skipping the parentheses and multiplying first would give a completely different, wrong answer. Whatever is grouped inside parentheses always happens first.

Test Tactic

"Add [something] to m, then multiply the result by [a number]" is written as [number](m + [something]) — the grouping symbol is what tells you the addition happens before the multiplication.

Math I Do

Pattern 4: Reading the Whole Chart

Real sheet music mixes rates, extra beats, and grouped expressions together. The skill FAST actually tests is building the RIGHT expression from the words before doing any arithmetic.

"[Number] beats per measure" → multiply the rate by the variable.

"[Number] more beats added" → add a fixed number, no multiplication.

"Add [something], then multiply" → use parentheses to group the addition first.

Test Tactic

"Per measure," "each measure," and "times as many" all signal multiplication. "More," "extra," and "added on" signal addition — mixing these up is where most points get lost.

ELA · ELA.6.R.3.4 I Do

Three Ways to Make a Case

A strong persuasive speech usually leans on three different kinds of rhetorical appeals.

ETHOS builds credibility. "As a science teacher with fifteen years in this building, I've seen firsthand what hands-on learning does for students."

PATHOS appeals to emotion. "Picture a kid who's never grown anything, watching their first tomato turn red."

LOGOS appeals to logic and evidence. "Schools with gardens see measurably higher engagement in science class."

As you read Malik's speech, watch for all three — most strong speeches use each one at a different point to do a different job.

ELA · ELA.6.R.1.2 I Do

Logos, and What the Speech Really Teaches

Logos is the appeal built on facts and numbers — the kind of evidence you could check. But underneath all three appeals, a speech usually has one bigger idea holding it together: its theme.

Malik's speech isn't just "Odessa Marsh was a good teacher." Read closely, and the bigger idea is that small, consistent effort, kept up over a long time, can build something that outlasts a single lifetime.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.4 I Do

Matching the Strongest Evidence

A speech's conclusion is only as convincing as the single strongest piece of evidence behind it. When FAST asks which detail best supports an argument, look for the most specific, concrete proof — not just any detail that's related.

Malik's core argument is that the room should be named after Odessa because of her lasting impact. Several details in the speech are related to her — but the strongest proof of "lasting impact" specifically is the concrete number of students who became professional musicians, not a general description of how people feel remembering her.
