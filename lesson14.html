Strand 1 · Number Sense & Operations I Do

The Temperature Swing

The festival opened at 8°F and had dropped to −6°F by closing time.

Total temperature drop = 8 − (−6) = 8 + 6 = 14 degrees. Subtracting a negative number is the same as adding its positive.

Test Tactic

To find the distance between two temperatures (or any two signed numbers), subtract them and take the absolute value — the sign of your answer should always describe distance, so it's never negative.

Strand 2 · Algebraic Reasoning I Do

Balancing the Festival Budget

The festival raised x dollars in ticket sales. After $150 in setup costs, they netted $650:

x − 150 = 650, so x + 150 − 150... to isolate x, undo the subtraction by adding 150 to both sides: x = 650 + 150 = $800.

Test Tactic

The same "opposite operation" move from earlier in the season still applies — whatever is attached to the variable, undo it on both sides.

Strand 3 · Geometric Reasoning I Do

Measuring the Main Stage

The main stage is a rectangle (20 ft by 15 ft) with a triangular awning extension (base 20 ft, height 6 ft) attached to one side.

Rectangle: 20 × 15 = 300 sq ft. Triangle: (20 × 6) ÷ 2 = 60 sq ft. Total area = 300 + 60 = 360 square feet.

Test Tactic

Composite figures always break into shapes you already know — find each piece separately, then add. It's the same move whether the shape is a garden bed, a stage, or a trophy case.

Strand 4 · Data & Probability I Do

Five Years of Festival Attendance

A box plot of attendance over the last five festivals: Min=200, Q1=350, Median=500, Q3=650, Max=900.

Range = Max − Min = 900 − 200 = 700 people — the full spread of attendance across five years.

Test Tactic

Range always uses the two outer values. It's the same rule whether the box plot tracks ages, donations, or attendance.

Hometown Wrap-Up

By dusk, the town square looked like every storyline from the season had shown up at once. Marcus and Jalen ran a football-toss booth. Jasmine signed a stack of Winter Classic photos. Nadia's beaded bracelets sold out before the fireworks even started.

None of it would have happened without months of unglamorous work — practices nobody filmed, garden beds nobody photographed, a council vote that took three years to earn. The festival wasn't really celebrating one big win. It was celebrating everything that built up to it.

The string lights over the square glowed like a whole galaxy had come down to visit for one night. Even the old oak tree in the corner seemed to lean in and listen to the speeches. A banner across the newly named music room read simply: BUILT BY SOMEONE LIKE YOU.

Coach Whitfield always said the same thing after every game, win or lose: the score tells you what happened, not why it happened. Standing in the square that night, watching a season's worth of quiet work turn into one loud, lit-up evening, that finally made sense.

ELA · ELA.6.R.2.3 / R.3.1 I Do

Why the Details, and Two More Devices

The banner isn't a random detail — it echoes something the season already argued for. And the passage uses BOTH a simile and personification in the same paragraph, so read carefully to tell them apart.

Simile: compares using "like" or "as." Personification: gives a non-human thing (like a tree) a human action, with no "like" or "as" needed.

Two Last Words

Strategy — Context clues.

"months of unglamorous work — practices nobody filmed, garden beds nobody photographed" — the examples that follow point to what "unglamorous" means: plain, not exciting to look at.

"a council vote that took three years to earn" — paired with "three years," "earn" points to achieving something through sustained effort, not receiving it for free.
